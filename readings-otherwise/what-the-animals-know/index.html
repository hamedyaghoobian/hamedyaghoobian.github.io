What the Animals Know

Nasrollah Monshi, Kalila wa Dimna (کلیله و دمنه); Attar, The Conference of the Birds; Ferdowsi, Shahnameh; Rumi, Masnavi; Varavini, Marzban-nameh; Parvin E'tesami, Divan / 2026

Animals in six books, lit by the mind each text gives them. 12,413 animals, 17,989 readings

1–6Read

Alone

Persian literature is full of animals. In Kalila wa Dimna, the book of fables that came from India through Middle Persian and Arabic and that Nasrollah Monshi set in Persian prose around 1144, two jackals scheme at the court of a lion. In Attar's Conference of the Birds the birds of the world argue their way to the Simorgh. In Ferdowsi's Shahnameh heroes ride, hunt and are called lions. In Rumi's Masnavi a hare outwits a lion, a parrot sends word to India, and the self is a donkey that will not be led. Two more came after: Varavini's Marzban-nameh, a book of counsel for princes told by animals at court, and the Divan of Parvin E'tesami, who in the 1930s set the ant against the snake and the cat against the mouse to argue about justice. Here are the six books with every word taken out but the animals, 12,413 of them, each lit where it falls.

The light comes from Steven Wagschal's Minding Animals in the Old and New Worlds (2018). Wagschal argues that people think about an animal's mind according to what they do with it and how close they live to it, more than according to what the animal can do: the rider and the horse, the hunter and the hunted, the farmer and the herd. He lays the possibilities out from no mind at all, the animal as a thing, through an animal that does what its kind does, to a mind read truly from the animal's own senses and nature, and, at the far end, human faculties given to it: speech, argument, cunning. Here the cream is stronger the more mind the passage gives, and yellow marks the rare mind read truly from the animal, the one Wagschal is looking for. Persian also runs the other way, and makes people into animals; an animal said of a person is drawn as an outline, an animal the text does not mean.

The books make different shapes. Kalila wa Dimna is bright: 78 per cent of its animals are given human minds, and the jackals, almost all of them, 98 per cent. The animals of the Shahnameh are faint: 61 per cent have no mind at all, they are counted, given, loaded and ridden, and 35 per cent only do what their kind does. And 36 per cent of its animal words are not animals: the lion appears 711 times as a man. Attar's book is half figures of speech, and its speaking birds are guides.

Rumi's Masnavi goes furthest. Two thirds of its animal words are not animals at all: the soul is a bird, the self a donkey, lust an ant that habit turns into a snake. No book here uses animals more to think about people. Yet when an animal in the Masnavi is an animal, it is more often given a mind read truly from it than anywhere else, 12 per cent, and Rumi's dogs most of all, a quarter of them.

The two later books settle two questions. Marzban-nameh, made two centuries after Kalila and on its model, is nearly as bright: 68 per cent of its animals, the bear, the camel, the mouse, are given human minds. The minds belong to the fable at court, not to one book. And Parvin E'tesami, the one modern poet here and the one woman, gives her animals a mind read from them more often than anyone: 15 per cent, eight hundred years after Ferdowsi's cavalry. Her ant, her hen, her wolf at the fold feel what such creatures feel, even while they argue like people.

Wagschal found in Spanish writing that horses and dogs were given the most mind and fish the least. Persian epic turns this round. Of 1,438 horses, two thirds are things, and only 3 per cent are given a mind read from the horse, fewer than the fish, which swim through fables. Of the 41 horses that are given any mind, 19 have names: Rakhsh above all, who guards Rostam asleep and fights the lion, and Shabrang Behzad and Shabdiz. It is the closeness of one rider to one horse that makes a mind, as Wagschal says; the rest are cavalry.

Choose a book with 1 to 6, and press h, r, t, n or f to see one kind of mind alone. Come close and the words can be read, with the text around them faint; point at an animal, or step through them with j and k, for its passage and how it was judged. The atlas counts it all, animal by animal. The animals drawn beside the readings are from NaarDoon, the open library of Iranian visual elements, each traced by hand from an object in a museum.

How it was read. The animal words were found with a list made by hand, 181 forms, in the endings and spellings of the old texts. Each of the 17,989 matches was then read in its passage by Claude Sonnet 5.5, a language model, under a written scheme of Wagschal's categories: whether the word is an animal at all (شیر is lion or milk, گور onager or grave), whether it is a creature, a character or a figure of speech, the relation to people, the faculties given, and where the mind falls. Of 140 judgements drawn at random and read again, 128 agreed on everything, two were wrong and ten arguable; the two were corrected. The scheme, the judgements and the corrections are in the code, to be read and argued with.
