Readings, Otherwise No. 4

What the Animals Know

Nasrollah Monshi, Kalila wa Dimna; Attar, The Conference of the Birds; Ferdowsi, Shahnameh / 2026

Read

Persian literature is full of animals. In Kalila wa Dimna, the book of fables that came from India through Middle Persian and Arabic and that Nasrollah Monshi set in Persian prose around 1144, two jackals scheme at the court of a lion. In Attar's Conference of the Birds the birds of the world argue their way to the Simorgh. In Ferdowsi's Shahnameh heroes ride, hunt and are called lions. Here are the three books with every word taken out but the animals, 7,254 of them, each lit where it falls.

The light comes from Steven Wagschal's Minding Animals in the Old and New Worlds (2018). Wagschal argues that people think about an animal's mind according to what they do with it and how close they live to it, more than according to what the animal can do: the rider and the horse, the hunter and the hunted, the farmer and the herd. He lays the possibilities out from no mind at all, the animal as a thing, through an animal that does what its kind does, to a mind read truly from the animal's own senses and nature, and, at the far end, human faculties given to it: speech, argument, cunning. Each animal here is lit by where its passage falls. Persian also runs the other way, and makes people into animals; an animal said of a person is drawn in a colour of its own.

The three books make three different shapes. Kalila wa Dimna is bright: 78 per cent of its animals are given human minds, and the jackals, almost all of them, 98 per cent. The animals of the Shahnameh are dim: 61 per cent have no mind at all, they are counted, given, loaded and ridden, and 35 per cent only do what their kind does. And 36 per cent of its animal words are not animals: the lion appears 711 times as a man. Attar's book is half figures of speech, and its speaking birds are guides.

Wagschal found in Spanish writing that horses and dogs were given the most mind and fish the least. Persian epic turns this round. Of 1,438 horses, two thirds are things, and only 3 per cent are given a mind read from the horse, fewer than the fish, which swim through fables. Of the 41 horses that are given any mind, 19 have names: Rakhsh above all, who guards Rostam asleep and fights the lion, and Shabrang Behzad and Shabdiz. It is the closeness of one rider to one horse that makes a mind, as Wagschal says; the rest are cavalry.

Choose a book above, and press a colour to see only those animals. Come close and the words can be read, with the text around them faint; point at an animal for its passage and how it was judged. The atlas counts it all, animal by animal.

How it was read. The animal words were found with a list made by hand, 181 forms, in the endings and spellings of the old texts. Each of the 10,457 matches was then read in its passage by Claude Sonnet 5.5, a language model, under a written scheme of Wagschal's categories: whether the word is an animal at all (شیر is lion or milk, گور onager or grave), whether it is a creature, a character or a figure of speech, the relation to people, the faculties given, and where the mind falls. Of 60 judgements drawn at random and read again, 54 agreed on everything, two were wrong and four arguable; the two were corrected. The scheme, the judgements and the corrections are in the code, to be read and argued with.

Texts: Kalila wa Dimna, Manteq al-Tayr and the Shahnameh on Ganjoor.
