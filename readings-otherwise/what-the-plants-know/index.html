What the Plants Know

Nasrollah Monshi, Kalila wa Dimna (کلیله و دمنه); Attar, The Conference of the Birds; Ferdowsi, Shahnameh; Rumi, Masnavi; Varavini, Marzban-nameh; Parvin E'tesami, Divan; Saadi, Golestan and Bustan / 2026

Plants in eight books, lit by the soul each text gives them. 5,202 plants, 6,628 readings

1–8Read

Alone

Persian literature is a garden. The beloved is a cypress, a cheek a rose or a tulip, the eyes narcissi, the hair hyacinth; the nightingale sings to the rose; Saadi called his two books the Rose Garden and the Orchard. What the Animals Know took six books of Persian literature and kept only the animals. Here are the same six, Kalila wa Dimna, Attar's Conference of the Birds, Ferdowsi's Shahnameh, Rumi's Masnavi, Varavini's Marzban-nameh and Parvin E'tesami's Divan, with Saadi's Golestan and Bustan beside them, and with every word taken out but the plants: 5,202 of them, each lit where it falls.

The light is the tradition's own ladder. Avicenna, after Aristotle, divided the soul in three: the vegetative soul that feeds and grows, which plants have; the animal soul, which adds sense, feeling and desire; the rational soul, which adds speech and reason and is human. Rumi made the ladder into a poem in the third book of the Masnavi: از جمادی مردم و نامی شدم / وز نما مردم به حیوان برزدم, I died as mineral and became a plant, I died as a plant and rose to animal. Each plant here is placed on that ladder by its passage: a thing, wood, fuel, fodder, a scent, faintest; plant life only, growing, blooming, withering; lifted to the feelings of the animal soul, laughing, weeping, longing; lifted to the speech and reason of the human soul, brightest. Yellow is a rung the old ladder does not have and plant science now insists on, a sense read truly from the plant: the date palm thirsting in a drought, the grass drunk beside the stream. A plant said of a person is an outline.

Most of the plants are not plants. More than half, 2,887 of 5,202, are figures of speech: the cypress is a figure four times in five, the rose half the time, the seed is nearly always a deed or a lineage. Of the plants that are plants, 40 per cent are things and 49 per cent only grow. The rest is thin, and almost none of it is yellow: twelve plants in eight books are given a sense true to what plants do. What botanists call plant blindness, the habit of not seeing plants as alive, is here as a shape. The poems skip the middle of the ladder. A plant is wood or a flower, or it is a person.

When it is a person, it is nearly always in Parvin. Of the 178 plants in these books given speech or reason, 113 are hers: the rose answering the thorn's sneer, the bud speaking to the withered flower, the tulip lecturing the violet, arguing over pride, beauty and death. Her book has the fewest figures of speech, 36 per cent, and the most plants that are plants. The Shahnameh has one speaking tree, and gives it everything: the talking tree that tells Alexander his death is near. Rumi's reed, cut from the reed-bed and complaining of the separation, opens the Masnavi. Saadi's Bustan, the Orchard, gives none of its plants a feeling or a voice.

Wagschal's claim for animals was that a creature's mind follows what people do with it. Plants answer him plainly. Plants eaten, used or given are things nine times in ten. Plants in a garden, tended and looked at, are given a human soul 18 per cent of the time, against 5 per cent of wild ones; only the emblems, the speaking trees of wonder, are given more.

Choose a book with 1 to 8, and press h, a, s, g, t or f to see one rung alone. Come close and the words can be read, with the text around them faint; point at a plant, or step through them with j and k, for its passage and how it was judged. The atlas counts it all, plant by plant. The plants drawn beside the readings are from NaarDoon, the open library of Iranian visual elements, each traced by hand from a Sasanian object or a Persian miniature.

How it was read. The plant words were found with a list made by hand, 113 forms, in the endings and spellings of the old texts; a plant's part counts, a leaf, a thorn, a grain, wood, but not what is made from it, wine, bread or rosewater, nor a place, a garden or a meadow. Each of the 6,628 matches was then read in its passage by Claude Sonnet 5.5, a language model, under a written scheme of the ladder: whether the word is a plant at all (گل is rose or mud, نی reed or no, سرو cypress or the king of Yemen), whether it is a plant, a character or a figure of speech, the relation to people, the faculties given, and the rung. 1,426 turned out not to be plants. Of 60 judgements drawn at random and read again, 56 agreed on everything, one was wrong and three arguable; the wrong one and three more found along the way were corrected. The scheme, the judgements and the corrections are in the code, to be read and argued with.

Texts: Kalila wa Dimna, Manteq al-Tayr, the Shahnameh, the Masnavi, Marzban-nameh, Parvin's Divan, the Golestan and the Bustan on Ganjoor. Frame: Avicenna's three souls; Rumi, Masnavi III, section 187; Michael Marder, Plant-Thinking: A Philosophy of Vegetal Life (Columbia University Press, 2013); Paco Calvo with Natalie Lawrence, Planta Sapiens (2022); James Wandersee and Elisabeth Schussler on "plant blindness" (1999); Annemarie Schimmel, A Two-Colored Brocade: The Imagery of Persian Poetry (University of North Carolina Press, 1992).
