What the Poets Answered

Every ghazal on Ganjoor, from Rudaki to the twentieth century / 2026

Ghazals as fireflies. 62,744 ghazals by 161 poets, 1,989 answers

Also show

A ghazal is a short lyric, five to fifteen couplets in one metre, every couplet closing on the same rhyme and often on the same refrain. For as long as Persian poets wrote ghazals they wrote them to one another. A poet would take up an earlier ghazal, in its metre and its rhyme, often its opening words, and write it again. They called it استقبال, going out to meet it, or جواب, an answer, or تتبع, following. Jami answered Hafez; Navai answered him seventy times; Saib answered forty-two of the poets before him.

This is every ghazal on Ganjoor by a poet who died before 1976: 62,744 of them, by 161 poets, from Rudaki in the 900s to the last century. Each is a firefly, set left to right by when its poet lived; up and down mean nothing. Each flashes on its own beat. When a ghazal answers an earlier one, it falls into step with it, and from then on the two flash together, centuries apart, a line between them. Answers to answers make chains, and a chain flashes as one. Press play and the centuries go by.

Fireflies in the mangroves of Southeast Asia flash together in their thousands. No one leads. Each charges and flashes on its own clock, and a neighbour's flash pushes it a little sooner, until the whole tree keeps one beat. Poets are a strange case of it: the pull reaches across centuries, and only the dead can pull.

Sharing a metre and a rhyme proves nothing by itself. Persian has only so many rhymes, and two ghazals share both about as often as they would if rhymes were dealt out at random. What holds is the opening. A ghazal is counted as an answer when its opening couplet takes up two or more of an earlier ghazal's words, outside the rhyme, in the same metre and rhyme, by a poet who died at least thirty lunar years later. 1,989 ghazals do. When the rhymes are shuffled, the same test finds about 450, averaged over ten shuffles, so about three in four of these are real; 195 of them say so in their title. Another 331 pairs are by poets too close in time to tell who answered whom, and 270 openings are the same word for word, a quotation or one poem carried in two divans. They are left dark unless you ask for them.

The strip at the foot counts it, century by century: the bar is the share of ghazals that answer an earlier poet, the line is chance. In the 1100s it is 4 of 1,886. By the 1500s it is one ghazal in twenty, and in the 1900s one in fifteen, while chance stays at two in a hundred or fewer. The field falls into step slowly and never all the way. In the 1600s, the century of Saib's 6,977 ghazals and the new Indian style, it falls back to one in fifty.

Saib is the poet answered most often, 197 times, but out of 6,977. Hafez is answered 155 times out of 544 ghazals, more than one in four; Saadi 127 times out of 708. The single ghazal answered most is Attar's عشق را اندر دو عالم هیچ پذرفتار نیست, six times, and its chain runs to nine ghazals, through Saadi, Navai and Hatef to Toghrol Ahrari in 1920. The longest reach is Rudaki's بوی جوی مولیان آید همی, answered by Rumi three centuries later, its chain reaching Bolandeqbal in 1900. Attar's birds set out each at their own pace and arrive as one. The poets mostly flash alone.

Press play, or space; ← and → move twenty years, 1 to 9 go to a century, 0 to the end. Point at a light, or press j and k to go through the answers in the order they were written, to read the opening and what it answers.

Text, metres and rhymes: Ganjoor, which tags every poem with its metre and its rhyme letters. Only poems Ganjoor files as ghazals and tags with a metre and a rhyme, by poets with a year of death; poets who died less than fifty years ago are left out. Dates are years of death; each ghazal is placed at random within its poet's last decades, since most ghazals are not dated. The model: Renato Mirollo and Steven Strogatz, “Synchronization of pulse-coupled biological oscillators” (1990), and Erik Fransson's firefly simulation. Made in 2026 with Claude, a language model.
