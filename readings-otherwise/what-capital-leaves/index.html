What Capital Leaves

Karl Marx, Capital, Volumes I–III, 1867–1894 / 2026

Punctuation on screen. … marks, … tokens

The machine is starting.

The machine has stopped. It will start again.

Every word of the three volumes has been taken out. What is left is the punctuation, laid down in reading order and painted as a sea, after Turner's Slave Ship (1840). Volume I, production, is the sky. Volume II, circulation, is where the water starts. Volume III, the process as a whole, is the deep.

From a distance it reads as wealth, one surface, an "immense accumulation of commodities." Come closer and the currents settle into marks, and the marks into the order in which Marx wrote them. Stay still and the words that were taken out come back up through the water.

The pale lights are quotation marks. In Capital these are the places where other people speak: factory inspectors, physicians, and the children who worked through the night, in their own words, in Marx's footnotes. Touch one.

A machine is reading it. A red light, the only red in the picture, moves through the marks in order, from the first sentence of Volume I to the last of Volume III, at about the speed a language model reads, and the words it passes over run along the top of the frame. It does not need you for this.

You can stop the machine. It starts again.

Press ≋ in the corner to take the painting apart: the ground, the marks, the voices, the ghosts. Then press 1 to 4 to see one of them alone.
