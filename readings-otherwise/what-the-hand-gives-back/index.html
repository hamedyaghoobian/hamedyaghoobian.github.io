What the Hand Gives Back

Mirza Abolqasem Farahani, called Qaem-Maqam, was born in 1779 in Farahan, near Arak. He served the crown prince Abbas Mirza through the wars with Russia and in 1834 became chief minister of Qajar Iran, but he is remembered less for that than for his letters. Persian official prose had grown so ornate that a letter could take a page to say good morning. His did not. He wrote short sentences, plain words and a little rhymed prose for wit, and the next century's writers took his letters as the model of how Persian should be written. In 1835 the new shah, Mohammad Shah, had him strangled in the Negarestan garden in Tehran.

His letters were collected after his death as the Monsha'at, منشآت, the writings, and printed. That is how they survive: as type. The sheets he and his scribes wrote on are scattered or gone, and the hand they were written in went with them. What reaches us is the words with the hand taken out.

This piece puts a hand back. It takes the 123 Persian letters of the Monsha'at as volunteers typed them for Ganjoor and writes each one out again, stroke by stroke, right to left, in a hand that never existed. Each word is shaped by HarfBuzz in the nasta'liq of Noto Nastaliq Urdu. Each letter of the font is thinned to the single line a pen would take, and repaired where the machine traces it wrongly: a م that came out as a dot, a ح that closed into a ه. The pen then travels that line at the speed a hand moves, slower in the turns. Its ink pools where it slows and thins where it hurries.

There are two hands. The first is a reed, قلم نی, of the kind Qaem-Maqam's secretaries used, measured against a Qajar letter in Harvard's Women's Worlds in Qajar Iran archive: its weight, the height of its letters, how many words fit a line. It draws the font's own thick and thin, revealed as the pen passes, and bends each word a little so no two are alike. The second is a fine pen, small, flat and quick, with the lines far apart, after the many handwritten letters of the religious scholars of the last century. Neither is anyone's hand. The words are Qaem-Maqam's; the hand is a guess at what was taken out.

Long letters are written as their opening, to the end of a sentence. Press n for another letter, h for the other hand. The same letter never comes out the same way twice.

Under the letter is a sheet for a reply. Write in Persian and the same hand writes it back, a word at a time, once each word is finished, since a Persian letter changes its shape until the word ends. Take a word back and it is crossed out, not erased: once, then harder, then scribbled over. The page can be kept.

Text: Qaem-Maqam Farahani, Monsha'at, the Persian letters as typed for Ganjoor. Shaped with HarfBuzz in Noto Nastaliq Urdu (SIL Open Font License). The ink is a density field after Rafael Cespedes's If This Finds You (MIT): strokes merge within a pass and add across passes, and the colour is the light they let through. The reed is measured against a letter in Women's Worlds in Qajar Iran (Harvard); the fine pen follows the handwritten letters of religious scholars of the last century. No text from any of them is used. Made in 2026 with Claude, a language model, which writes Persian without ever holding a pen.
