Readings, Otherwise No. 2

What the Birds Leave

Farid ud-Din Attar, Manteq al-Tayr (منطق‌الطیر), c. 1177 / 2026

Dots in flight. 112,052 dots, 4,697 couplets, one metre

Set in

In Persian script many letters share one body and are told apart only by their dots. Read from the right, ب پ ت ث are one stroke with one, three, two and three dots. Take the strokes away and what is left of a book is a scattering of small diamonds, each one the print of a reed pen pressed once against the page.

This is the whole of The Conference of the Birds, all 4,697 couplets, with every letter removed and only the dots kept. They are taken from the poem as the machine sets it: each line shaped in a typeface, each dot lifted from exactly where it falls. Then they are let go.

Farid ud-Din Attar was born around 1145 in Nishapur, in Khorasan, in the northeast of what is now Iran. In his time it was one of the great cities of the eastern Islamic world, on the road between Baghdad and Central Asia. Attar is a pen name taken from his trade. An attar was a herbalist and druggist, and since most medicine then was made from plants, he was something like a town doctor and pharmacist in one. He kept a pharmacy, and says he wrote some of his poems there, among the patients who came to him each day. Besides his long poems he wrote a book of the lives of the Sufi saints. He died around 1221, the year the Mongols destroyed Nishapur, and the story is that he was killed when the city fell.

He finished The Conference of the Birds in 1177. Its Persian title, منطق‌الطیر, Manteq al-Tayr, the speech of the birds, comes from the Quran, where Solomon is taught the language of birds. The birds of the world gather because they have no king. The hoopoe, once Solomon's messenger, tells them they have one, the Simorgh, who lives beyond the mountains at the edge of the world. One by one they make excuses. The nightingale cannot leave the rose, the parrot wants the water of life, the peacock wants paradise, the hawk is content on the wrist of kings. The hoopoe answers each of them with stories, and most of the poem is those stories. The longest is about Sheikh San'an, a holy man who gives up everything for love of a Christian girl.

Attar was a Sufi, and the journey is the soul's journey toward God. The hoopoe leads the birds through seven valleys: quest, love, knowledge, detachment, unity, bewilderment, and last poverty and annihilation, where the self is lost. Most of the birds give up or die along the way. Thirty arrive, and in the presence of the Simorgh they see only themselves, thirty birds, سی مرغ, si morgh. The whole poem is built to reach that one pun. What they crossed the world to find was not somewhere else, and the way to it was the losing of themselves.

The poem is one of the great works of Persian Sufi poetry, between Sanai before it and Rumi after. A later story has the young Rumi meet the old Attar in Nishapur, and Rumi is remembered as saying that Attar had passed through the seven cities of love while he was still at the turn of one street. The poem was copied and painted for centuries; one of the most famous copies, begun in Herat in 1487 and finished in Isfahan a century later, is in the Metropolitan Museum. Navai retold it in Turkic in 1499, and Peter Brook put it on the stage in 1979.

The poem can be set in two hands. Naskh is the book hand, upright and even. Nastaliq is the hand Persian poetry was copied in, the words sloping down to the left, the dots heavier, pressed with a wider pen. Change the hand and the same 112,052 dots take off and come down somewhere else. It is the same poem.

Or read it in English. Edward FitzGerald, who made Omar Khayyam famous in English, also made a version of this poem: Bird-Parliament, 1,438 lines, shortened and rhymed, published in 1889 after his death. English has almost no dots. Only i and j carry one, and where an f runs into an i the printer's ligature swallows it. FitzGerald's poem has 2,840. Choose it and the same 112,052 birds set out, but only 2,840 have anywhere to land. The rest scatter and are gone. FitzGerald spells the king Symurgh, with a y, so in English the Simorgh has no dots at all; the only gold left is the dot over the i of Thirty.

Drag and the dots take flight. Stand still and they come home, line by line, right to left, the way the poem is read, and the letters they belong to show faintly beneath them. Come close enough and you can read. Bring your pointer near a resting line and the birds lift off it.

Press ≋ in the corner to take the flock apart. The letters lie at the bottom. Above them the dots are sorted by the letters they came from: one dot, two, three. Then the dots of پ, چ and ژ, the letters Persian added when it took the Arabic alphabet for itself, which have no place in Arabic. About one dot in nine is there only because the poem is Persian. Choose a layer and the flock is put back together with only those birds in it, to go through on their own.

The gold dots are the ones that belong to the Simorgh, سیمرغ, wherever its name is written, and to the eight places where Attar splits it into سی مرغ, thirty birds. There are 164 of them in the flock.
