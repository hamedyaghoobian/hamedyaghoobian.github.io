## Week 1 · Bias and variance

A model that is too simple misses the pattern; one that is too flexible memorises the noise. The test error tells them apart: it falls as the model gains flexibility, then climbs again once the model starts fitting noise.

{% include project-figure.html id="w1-figure" script="csi330-w1-figure.js" class="project-figure--study" calc="1" fig="Week 1" title="The complexity dial" hint="degree selector · ← →" note="Simulated data: a noisy sine. The training error only falls; the test error is the one to watch." %}

<div class="study-try" markdown="1">
Try this

1. Start at degree 1. Before you move it, predict: as the degree rises, which error keeps falling and which one turns back up?
2. Find the degree with the lowest **test** error.
3. Go to degree 12. Its training error is the lowest of all. Would you choose it?

<details markdown="1"><summary>What you should see</summary>

Training error only falls (0.548 at degree 1, 0.256 at degree 12): more flexibility can always bend closer to the points it was fitted on. Test error falls to its lowest, **0.357 at degree 5**, then rises sharply: 1.848 at degree 11 and 4.42 at degree 12. Degree 12 has memorised the noise in its 20 training points. That is why a model is never chosen by its training error.
</details>
</div>

## Week 2 · k-nearest neighbors

To classify a bird it has never seen, k-NN finds the k most similar birds it has and lets them vote. Two choices shape the answer: how many neighbours vote, and how distance is measured. Bill length spans about 28 mm and bill depth about 9 mm, so in raw millimetres length dominates; standardising puts both on the same footing.

{% include project-figure.html id="w2-figure" script="csi330-w2-figure.js" class="project-figure--study" calc="1" fig="Week 2" title="The neighbour board" hint="move over the board · ←→↑↓ · K for k · S for units" note="All 342 penguins. Standardised distance is what a StandardScaler pipeline uses." %}

<div class="study-try" markdown="1">
Try this

1. Move the query to about 38 × 14 mm with k = 5. Read the vote in raw millimetres, then press **S** for standardised.
2. Set k = 1 and move slowly through the middle of the board, where the species mix. Then try k = 25.
3. Why does an even k make trouble for a vote?

<details markdown="1"><summary>What you should see</summary>

1. At 38 × 14 mm, raw millimetres gives **5 Adelie** votes and standardised gives **5 Gentoo**: a complete reversal. Bill depth varies much less than length (a standard deviation of 1.97 mm against 5.45 mm), so 1 mm of depth is far more unusual than 1 mm of length. Raw millimetres ignores that and lets length decide.
2. With k = 1 the prediction flips with every bird you pass (high variance). With k = 25 it changes slowly and smooths over the mixed region, but it can be outvoted by a bigger class nearby (more bias). It's the same dial as week 1.
3. Two classes can tie. This page breaks ties toward the nearest neighbour's class; an odd k avoids ties between two classes.
</details>
</div>

## Week 3 · Gaussian naive Bayes

Naive Bayes asks which species makes this measurement most likely, and weighs that by how common the species is. Each class gets a bell curve; the prediction is the class with the largest prior × likelihood. Where two curves are about the same height, the prior decides.

{% include project-figure.html id="w3-figure" script="csi330-w3-figure.js" class="project-figure--study" calc="1" fig="Week 3" title="The Bayes balance" hint="move over the curves · ← → · P priors" note="GaussianNB fitted on bill length for all 342 birds." %}

<div class="study-try" markdown="1">
Try this

1. Weigh birds at 42, 50 and 56 mm, each with both priors (**P**). For which length does the prior change the answer?
2. Adelie is the most common species (prior 0.442). At 50 mm, why does it get almost no weight?
3. In the calculation, compare Chinstrap's and Gentoo's likelihoods at 50 mm, then their priors.

<details markdown="1"><summary>What you should see</summary>

| bill length | priors from the data | equal priors |
|---|---|---|
| 42 mm | Adelie 0.72 | Adelie 0.64 |
| 50 mm | **Gentoo 0.60** | **Chinstrap 0.55** |
| 56 mm | Chinstrap 0.69 | Chinstrap 0.80 |

Only 50 mm flips. Adelie bills average 38.8 mm with a spread of 2.7 mm, so 50 mm is about four spreads away and the likelihood is almost zero: a large prior times nearly zero is still nearly zero. At 50 mm, Chinstrap's curve is a little taller than Gentoo's, but Gentoo's prior is almost twice as large (123 birds to 68), so with the data's priors Gentoo wins. With equal priors, only the heights count.
</details>
</div>

## Week 4 · Linear regression and least squares

A line predicts a number from a number. Each bird's residual is how far the line misses it, measured straight up or down. Least squares picks the line that makes the sum of the squared misses as small as possible, and that line always passes through the mean point.

{% include project-figure.html id="w4-figure" script="csi330-w4-figure.js" class="project-figure--study" calc="1" fig="Week 4" title="The spring-loaded line" hint="point to turn the rod · ← → · F to let it settle" note="Forty penguins; least squares fitted on those forty. A spring's energy grows with the square of its stretch, so resting springs find the least-squares line." %}

<div class="study-try" markdown="1">
Try this

1. Turn the rod flat (slope 0). What is this line predicting for every bird, and what is its RMSE?
2. Press **F** and let it settle. Where does it settle, and what is the RMSE now?
3. Turn the rod a little either way from the best slope. Which springs change the most?

<details markdown="1"><summary>What you should see</summary>

A flat rod through the pivot predicts the mean mass, 4,463 g, for everyone: RMSE **794 g**. The rod settles at **48.2 g per mm**, RMSE **390 g**, half the error of guessing the mean. The springs at the two ends stretch most when the rod turns, because they are farthest from the pivot (203 mm, 4,463 g). Points far from the mean flipper length have the most leverage over the slope.
</details>
</div>

## Week 5 · k-nearest neighbors for regression

For a number instead of a class, k-NN averages its neighbours instead of letting them vote. The same dial returns: k = 1 follows every bird, noise included; a very large k flattens toward one overall average.

{% include project-figure.html id="w5-figure" script="csi330-w5-figure.js" class="project-figure--study" calc="1" fig="Week 5" title="The averaging carriage" hint="move over the board · ← → flipper · ↑ ↓ k" note="All 342 penguins. The dashed trace is the full prediction curve for the chosen k; the faint line is week 4's least squares." %}

<div class="study-try" markdown="1">
Try this

1. Put the pointer at 200 mm and step k through 1, 5, 15 and 150 (**↑ ↓**). Write down each prediction.
2. Compare the dashed trace at k = 1 and at k = 150 with the faint straight line.
3. Why does k = 150 flatten the trace near the two ends of the axis?

<details markdown="1"><summary>What you should see</summary>

At 200 mm: k = 1 gives **4,050 g**, k = 5 gives 3,795 g, k = 15 gives 3,953 g and k = 150 gives 3,909 g. The least-squares line predicts 4,156 g. At k = 1 the trace jumps from bird to bird. At k = 150 it is smooth but too flat: near the ends, most of the 150 neighbours lie toward the middle, so the average is pulled back toward the overall mean (4,202 g). This is bias, and it's why k-NN struggles at the edges of the data.
</details>
</div>

## Week 6 · Classification metrics

A classifier that outputs probabilities still needs a threshold to say yes or no. Every threshold gives a different confusion matrix. Lower it and you catch more of the people who have diabetes (recall rises), but you flag more who don't (precision falls).

{% include project-figure.html id="w6-figure" script="csi330-w6-figure.js" class="project-figure--study" calc="1" fig="Week 6" title="The threshold sorter" hint="move over the board · ← → fine · ↑ ↓ coarse" note="NHANES: logistic regression on glucose, trained on 70% and scored on the 517 people held out." %}

<div class="study-try" markdown="1">
Try this

1. At threshold 0.50, read accuracy and recall. Is this a good screening test?
2. Read the "always says no" figure in the calculation. What does it tell you about accuracy here?
3. Lower the threshold to 0.20, then to 0.10. Watch FN and FP trade places.

<details markdown="1"><summary>What you should see</summary>

At 0.50: accuracy **0.86**, but recall only **0.50**, so it misses 58 of the 117 people with diabetes. Only 23% of the test set has diabetes, so a model that always says "no" already scores **0.77**: accuracy flatters a rare class. At 0.20, recall rises to 0.78 and precision falls to 0.64. At 0.10, recall is 0.92, but 190 people without diabetes are flagged (precision 0.36). For screening, a missed case (FN) usually costs more than a false alarm (FP), so a lower threshold is defensible. The right threshold depends on those costs, not on the model.
</details>
</div>

## Week 7 · Regression metrics

MAE, RMSE and R² summarise the same residuals in different ways. MAE is the typical size of a miss. RMSE squares the misses before averaging, so a few large ones dominate it. R² asks how much better the model does than always predicting the mean.

{% include project-figure.html id="w7-figure" script="csi330-w7-figure.js" class="project-figure--study" calc="1" fig="Week 7" title="The residual sticks" hint="drag a point · ← → pick · ↑ ↓ move · R reset" note="Thirty typical NHANES participants (insulin under 60, glucose under 12) against a line fitted on everyone." %}

<div class="study-try" markdown="1">
Try this

1. Press **R**, then write down MAE and RMSE.
2. Drag the rightmost point (glucose 10.5) up to an insulin of 150. Before you look, predict which metric grows more.
3. R² barely changes. Look at its formula in the calculation: why?

<details markdown="1"><summary>What you should see</summary>

Before: MAE **7.3**, RMSE **8.4**. After making one outlier: MAE **11.2** (up about half) and RMSE **24.5** (almost three times). One large miss, once squared, outweighs the other 29. R² only falls from 0.18 to 0.12: the outlier enlarges the squared error, but it also pulls the mean up and enlarges the spread around it (the denominator), so the ratio moves less. Report MAE and RMSE together: a big gap between them means a few large errors.
</details>
</div>

## Week 8 · Cross-validation

One train/test split gives one score, and a lucky or unlucky split can mislead. k-fold cross-validation rotates which part is held out, so every observation is tested once, and reports the average of k scores along with their spread.

{% include project-figure.html id="w8-figure" script="csi330-w8-figure.js" class="project-figure--study" calc="1" fig="Week 8" title="The fold tray" hint="click a fold · ← → rounds · ↑ ↓ k" note="Wine: logistic regression on alcohol and density, StratifiedKFold. One card stands for five wines; tabbed cards are red." %}

<div class="study-try" markdown="1">
Try this

1. With k = 5, step through all five rounds. What is the lowest fold score, and what is the highest?
2. If you had made a single 80/20 split, which of those numbers could you have reported?
3. Compare k = 3, 5 and 10: the mean, and the spread.

<details markdown="1"><summary>What you should see</summary>

With k = 5 the folds score from **0.65 to 0.80**, so a single split could have reported either. Cross-validation reports **0.725 ± 0.050** instead. The means barely move with k (0.740, 0.725, 0.725), but the spread grows (0.031, 0.050, 0.060), because each fold gets smaller (67, 40, then 20 wines) and a small test set gives noisier scores. Larger k trains on more data each round, at the cost of more rounds.
</details>
</div>
