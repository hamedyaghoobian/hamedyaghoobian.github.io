<p class="ro-intro" data-t="intro">Old texts, read with new machines. Not quite close reading, not quite distant reading. Something sideways. Each piece takes a book apart and keeps what usually gets thrown away. Speculative, unfinished, and mostly for looking at.</p>

      <p class="ro-row__line">Plants in eight books, lit by the soul each text gives them. 5,202 plants, 6,628 readings</p>
      <dl class="fp-spec ro-spec">

      <p class="ro-note">The six books of <em>What the Animals Know</em>, with Saadi's <em>Golestan</em>, <bdi lang="fa">گلستان</bdi>, and <em>Bustan</em> beside them, with every word taken out but the plants, each lit by how high the text lifts it up the tradition's own ladder: Avicenna's vegetative, animal and rational souls, and Rumi's <bdi lang="fa">از جمادی مردم و نامی شدم</bdi>, I died as mineral and became a plant. More than half the plants are not plants but people: the cypress is a stature, the rose a cheek. The rest are wood or flowers, and the poems skip the middle of the ladder: twelve plants in eight books are given a sense true to plants. When a plant speaks, it is nearly always in Parvin, whose rose answers the thorn; the <em>Shahnameh</em> has one talking tree, and it tells Alexander he will die.</p>

      <p class="ro-row__line">Animals in six books, lit by the mind each text gives them. 12,413 animals, 17,989 readings</p>
      <dl class="fp-spec ro-spec">

        <dt>left</dt><dd>12,413 animals in Kalila wa Dimna, the Conference of the Birds, the Shahnameh, the Masnavi, Marzban-nameh and Parvin E'tesami's Divan</dd>
        <dt>light</dt><dd>how much mind the text gives each one, after Wagschal's <em>Minding Animals</em></dd>
      </dl>
      <p class="ro-note">Six books of Persian literature, <em>Kalila wa Dimna</em>, <bdi lang="fa">کلیله و دمنه</bdi>, Attar's <em>Conference of the Birds</em>, Ferdowsi's <em>Shahnameh</em>, Rumi's <em>Masnavi</em>, Varavini's <em>Marzban-nameh</em> and Parvin E'tesami's <em>Divan</em>, with every word taken out but the animals, each lit by how much mind the text gives it, in the terms of Steven Wagschal's <em>Minding Animals</em>. The fables are bright: the jackals scheme with human minds. The epic is dim: its horses are counted, given and ridden, and its lions are mostly men. Of the horses given a mind, half have names. Rumi's animals are mostly people in disguise, but his dogs are given minds of their own; and Parvin, the one modern poet, reads her animals most truly of all.</p>

        <dt>left</dt><dd>6,409 words from Arabic, one in ninety</dd>

      <p class="ro-note">Ferdowsi finished the <em>Shahnameh</em>, <bdi lang="fa">شاهنامه</bdi>, the Book of Kings, in 1010: some 50,000 couplets of Iran's history from the first king to the Arab conquest, famous for keeping its Persian nearly free of Arabic. Here it is with every Persian word taken out. What is left are the 6,409 words he let in from Arabic, one in ninety, scattered across a dark field: Caesar, <bdi lang="fa">قیصر</bdi>, the rein, ivory, arms, the spearhead. Come close and they can be read in their lines. A second layer shades in red the 166 sections whose headings name a death.</p>

      <dl class="fp-spec ro-spec">

        <dt>gold</dt><dd><span class="ro-gold" aria-hidden="true"></span>164 dots that spell the Simorgh</dd>
      </dl>
      <p class="ro-note">In 1177 Attar of Nishapur, <bdi lang="fa">عطار نیشابوری</bdi>, a herbalist and druggist in Khorasan, finished <em>The Conference of the Birds</em>, <bdi lang="fa">منطق‌الطیر</bdi>, a Sufi poem in which the birds of the world set out to find their king, the Simorgh, <bdi lang="fa">سیمرغ</bdi>, and the thirty who survive the journey find only themselves: <bdi lang="fa">سی مرغ</bdi>, <em>si morgh</em>, thirty birds. Here it is with every letter taken out. The 112,052 dots that remain fly as a murmuration. Stand still and they come home to the verse, over the bare skeletons of the letters they were lifted from. Set it in naskh or nastaliq and the same dots land somewhere else. In FitzGerald's English only 2,840 have anywhere to land. The gold dots spell the Simorgh.</p>
